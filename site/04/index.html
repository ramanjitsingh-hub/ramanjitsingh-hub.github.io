Investigation 04 · Hybrid search

When does mixing keyword and meaning search help?

Luna remembers a founder's work by searching it two ways at once, by exact words and by meaning, and mixing the results. Mixing is widely assumed to help. On five public benchmarks it often does not. The most common recipe, reciprocal rank fusion, which Luna's memory itself uses with k = 60, makes results worse than meaning search alone on three of five. A weighted mix, with the weight learned on held-out queries, is never significantly worse and is significantly better on three. A two-signal model from detection theory predicts which way an equal mix will go from three numbers per dataset.

§1The question

Keyword search finds documents that share the query's words. Meaning search finds documents that say the same thing in other words. Each fails where the other succeeds, so mixing them sounds like a free improvement.

Luna's memory works this way. When a founder asks about "the pricing call with the Pune distributor", keyword search catches the exact name and meaning search catches the notes that never used the word "pricing". Many products mix the two with a fixed recipe and never measure whether the mix beats its better half. This page measures it, asks when it should help, and tests a model that answers in advance.

§2Two ways to search

Keyword search is BM25[2], written here from scratch on sparse matrices. A document \(D\) scores, for a query \(Q\),

$$\text{BM25}(D, Q) = \sum_{t \in Q} \log\!\Big(1 + \frac{N - n_t + 0.5}{n_t + 0.5}\Big) \frac{f_{t,D}\,(k_1 + 1)}{f_{t,D} + k_1\big(1 - b + b\,|D| / \overline{|D|}\big)},$$

where \(f_{t,D}\) counts term \(t\) in \(D\), \(n_t\) is the number of documents containing it, and \(k_1 = 0.9\), \(b = 0.4\). Meaning search encodes queries and documents with bge-small-en-v1.5[3], a 33-million-parameter encoder, on the laptop GPU, and scores by cosine similarity.

The five datasets cover scientific claims (SciFact), medical questions (NFCorpus), counter-arguments (ArguAna), citation prediction (SCIDOCS) and financial questions (FiQA). Quality is nDCG@10, which rewards placing relevant documents in the top ten, and higher places more. Every comparison is paired by query, with 10,000 bootstrap resamples for intervals, a sign-flip test for p-values, and Holm's correction across the five datasets[7].

Six mixes are compared. Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) adds \(1/(60 + \text{rank})\) from each list[4]. Min-max rescales each score to \([0,1]\) over its top 1,000 and adds them[5]. z-scores standardise each score, either over a random sample of the corpus or over the top 200 documents where ranking actually happens. Min-max and the top-200 z-score are each run with equal weights and with a weight on keyword search chosen by 5-fold cross-validation over queries. Bruch, Gai and Ingber analyse several of these functions in depth[6].

§3When mixing should help

Signal detection theory[8] gives a simple model. For one query, standardise both scores over the documents that are not relevant, so each has mean 0 and variance 1 there and the two have correlation \(\rho\). Relevant documents sit higher, at \(d_1\) on the keyword score and \(d_2\) on the meaning score. The larger the separation, the better a score ranks.

Proposition 1 · the separation of a mix

If both kinds of document are Gaussian with the same covariance \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), a mix \(w_1 s_1 + w_2 s_2\) has separation \(d(w) = w^\top d / \sqrt{w^\top \Sigma w}\), and the chance that a relevant document outscores a non-relevant one is \(\Phi(d(w)/\sqrt2)\). The best weights are \(w \propto \Sigma^{-1} d\), with

$$d_\ast^2 = \frac{d_1^2 + d_2^2 - 2\rho\, d_1 d_2}{1 - \rho^2}, \qquad d_\ast^2 - d_1^2 = \frac{(d_2 - \rho\, d_1)^2}{1 - \rho^2} \;\ge\; 0 .$$

An equal mix has separation \((d_1 + d_2)/\sqrt{2 + 2\rho}\), and beats the stronger score \(d_1 \ge d_2\) only when \(\rho < (d_1 + d_2)^2 / (2 d_1^2) - 1\).

proofThe difference of two independent draws, one relevant and one not, is Gaussian with mean \(w^\top d\) and variance \(2 w^\top \Sigma w\), which gives the probability. Maximising \(w^\top d / \sqrt{w^\top \Sigma w}\) is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in the inner product defined by \(\Sigma\), with equality at \(w \propto \Sigma^{-1} d\) and maximum \(d^\top \Sigma^{-1} d\), which expands to the first expression. Subtracting \(d_1^2\) and completing the square gives the second. The equal-mix condition is \((d_1 + d_2)^2 / (2 + 2\rho) > d_1^2\), rearranged. ∎

Two lessons follow before any data. A correctly weighted mix can never be worse than its better half, and it gains nothing exactly when \(\rho = d_2 / d_1\), when the weaker score adds no information the stronger lacks. An equal mix is different. When one score is much stronger, an equal mix drags it down unless the two are nearly uncorrelated or negatively correlated. Whether mixing helps is a question about \(d_1\), \(d_2\) and \(\rho\), not about the idea of mixing.

§6Does the model predict it

For each query I measured \(d_1\), \(d_2\) and \(\rho\) among the non-relevant documents in the top 200, standardised there, and used Proposition 1 to predict how well an equal mix separates relevant from non-relevant documents. The prediction uses only the two separate scores. The mix itself is then measured.
