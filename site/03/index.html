Investigation 03 · Startup statistics

What can a startup learn from fifty users?

At FemoraAI I read retention numbers every week, for cohorts of a few hundred new users. Founders decide on numbers like these all the time. This investigation measures what they can support. The whole customer base of a real retailer serves as the truth, and startup-sized samples are drawn from it. With 50 users a retention rate is known only to about ±11 points, a founder who checks a test every day finds a false winner 42% of the time, and two simple tools, shrinkage and always-valid tests, recover much of what the small numbers lose.

§1The question

An early-stage company runs on small numbers. Thirty sign-ups this week, nine of them still active a month later, a new onboarding flow that "lifted retention by six points".

Each of those statements hides a statistical question, and in my experience founders rarely ask it. How sure can thirty users make you? Is this month's cohort better than last month's, or just luckier? If you check an experiment every morning and stop when it looks good, how often are you fooling yourself? And what is the cheapest honest way to report a number?

These questions have textbook answers. The textbook assumes things a startup cannot check, so this page tests each answer on real behaviour. Real users arrive in uneven monthly cohorts, behave differently in different months, and do not come from any formula. The truth is known because the whole customer base is in the data, and every small sample is drawn from it.

§2A customer base as the truth

The data holds every purchase of a UK online gift retailer over 25 months, about a million lines. After removing cancellations and purchases with no customer identifier, each customer is placed in the cohort of the calendar month of their first purchase. The first month of data is dropped, because its "new" customers include everyone who had bought before the records began. The last two are dropped too, because their following month is incomplete.

The metric is next-month retention, the share of a cohort that buys again in the following calendar month. It is the retail version of "still active after four weeks". Across the · new customers it is ·, and that is the number every sample below tries to recover.

§3How wide is fifty users

Draw \(n\) customers at random, count the \(x\) who came back, and report \(\hat p = x/n\) with a 95% interval. The interval most people learn first is Wald's, \(\hat p \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\). Brown, Cai and DasGupta showed in 2001 that it behaves badly at small \(n\), and recommended Wilson's interval[2, 3], which comes from inverting the score test instead of plugging in \(\hat p\),

$$\frac{\hat p + \frac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}} .$$

Clopper and Pearson's exact interval[4] and the Bayesian Jeffreys interval are included for comparison. Each interval below was computed on 20,000 random samples at each size, and checked against the true rate.

§4Is this month really better

Figure 1 shows retention moving between months. Some of that is sampling noise, since a cohort of 150 is itself a small sample. Some may be real, from seasons, campaigns or a change in who is buying. The two can be separated. If every cohort had the same true rate \(p\), the statistic \(\sum_m (\hat p_m - \bar p)^2 / (\bar p(1-\bar p)/n_m)\) would follow a chi-squared distribution with one less degree of freedom than there are cohorts.

§5Borrowing strength

When every cohort is small, each one's raw rate is noisy, but the cohorts together say a lot about what a typical cohort looks like. Empirical Bayes uses that. Treat each cohort's true rate as drawn from a beta distribution, estimate the distribution from all cohorts at once, and report each cohort's posterior mean[5].

Proposition 1 · shrinkage as a weighted average

If \(p_m \sim \text{Beta}(a, b)\) and \(x_m \mid p_m \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p_m)\), the posterior mean is

$$\mathbb E[p_m \mid x_m] = \frac{x_m + a}{n + a + b} = w\,\hat p_m + (1-w)\,\frac{a}{a+b}, \qquad w = \frac{n}{n + a + b},$$

so each cohort's estimate moves toward the overall mean by a fraction that shrinks as the cohort grows. Choosing \(a, b\) to match the observed mean \(\bar p\) and the between-cohort variance \(\tau^2\) gives \(a + b = \bar p(1-\bar p)/\tau^2 - 1\).

proofThe beta prior is conjugate to the binomial, so the posterior is \(\text{Beta}(a + x_m, b + n - x_m)\), whose mean is the first expression. Rearranging gives the weighted average. A \(\text{Beta}(a,b)\) distribution has mean \(a/(a+b)\) and variance \(\mu(1-\mu)/(a+b+1)\). Setting these equal to \(\bar p\) and \(\tau^2\) gives the last formula. ∎

To test it, every cohort of at least 150 customers is cut down to \(n\) random customers, 2,000 times, as if each month had brought only \(n\) users. Each method then estimates every cohort's full rate.

§6The cost of checking every day

A product team ships two onboarding flows to alternate users and watches the dashboard. A z-test at the 5% level is valid if it is run once, at a sample size fixed in advance. Few teams work that way. They look every day and stop when the result is significant.

To measure what that costs, I ran A/A tests, where both arms are the same product, so every "significant" result is a false alarm. Customers arrive in their real order, month by month. Each replicate splits them at random into two arms and tests after every 10 users per arm, up to 2,000.

fig. 4  false alarms in identical arms against the number of times the test is checked

Figure 4. ·

The fix is a test built to be checked at any time. The mixture sequential probability ratio test[6] averages the likelihood ratio over a range of possible effects, an idea that goes back to Robbins[7], and stops when it passes \(1/\alpha\).

Proposition 2 · a test that can be checked at any time

With \(n\) users per arm, mean difference \(\bar\Delta_n\), per-pair variance \(\sigma^2\) and a \(\mathcal N(0, \tau^2)\) mixture over the true difference,

$$\Lambda_n = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2 + n\tau^2}}\, \exp\!\Big(\frac{n^2\tau^2\, \bar\Delta_n^2}{2\sigma^2(\sigma^2 + n\tau^2)}\Big).$$

If the arms are equal, \(P(\Lambda_n \ge 1/\alpha \text{ for some } n) \le \alpha\), however often the test is checked.

proofFor each fixed true difference \(\theta\), the likelihood ratio of \(\theta\) against 0 is a nonnegative martingale with mean 1 when the arms are equal. A mixture of such martingales over \(\theta\) is again one, and \(\Lambda_n\) is that mixture in closed form for Gaussian increments. Ville's inequality bounds the chance that a nonnegative martingale starting at 1 ever reaches \(1/\alpha\) by \(\alpha\). The same argument certified the router of investigation 02. ∎

§7How many users a change needs

The smallest change a test can reliably see, at 5% significance and 80% power, is about \((z_{0.975} + z_{0.8})\sqrt{2p(1-p)/n}\) for \(n\) users per arm.
