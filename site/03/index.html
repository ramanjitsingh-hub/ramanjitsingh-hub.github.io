Investigation 03 · GPU anatomy

The anatomy of a graphics card

Seven small experiments, each isolating one mechanism of a laptop GPU, and each tested against a simple model written down before it ran. Most models hold to within a few percent. Where one does not, the gap is measured and kept, because the gaps are where the hardware is more interesting than the textbook.

§1Method, and a moving clock

A GPU's speed is usually explained with a handful of rules. Coalesce your loads. Keep enough work in flight. Avoid divergent branches and bank conflicts. Each rule hides a quantitative model, and each model can be tested.

Every experiment below is one small kernel in src/anat.cu that varies a single parameter. The model is written down first, and only then is the sweep run. Earlier work in investigation 02 found a two-fold speed-up by noticing one of these effects in a hardware counter, which is the reason for studying them one at a time.

One complication appeared immediately. The clock is not fixed. Each kernel reads the SM cycle counter clock64() against the nanosecond timer %globaltimer in its first block, which gives the clock during that very launch. Two states appear. Under heavy memory or FP32 traffic the GPU holds its base clock of 1.207 GHz, and otherwise it boosts to about 1.80 GHz. Every peak on this page is computed at the clock measured in the same run, and latencies are reported in cycles.

§2Three latencies

One thread follows a chain of pointers through an array, so each load must finish before the next address is known. The chain is a single random cycle over 128-byte lines, built with Sattolo's algorithm, so neighbouring loads are never neighbours in memory. The time per load is then the latency of whichever memory level holds the array. The model is a staircase, one step per level.

fig. 1  cycles per dependent load against working-set size

Figure 1. ·

The steps are sharp. The L1 step ends between 92 KB and 131 KB and the L2 step between 741 KB and 1.48 MB, consistent with the 1 MB L2 the driver reports. Beyond 64 MB each load costs about 10 cycles more. A dedicated experiment in §9 traces it to a 16-entry address-translation cache with 2 MB pages, which covers exactly 32 MB.

§3Bandwidth needs bytes in flight

Memory bandwidth is not a property of the memory alone. A load that takes 291 ns to return must be overlapped with many others, and Little's law[3] from queueing theory says how many. If \(L\) is the latency and \(B\) the bandwidth, the number of bytes in flight must be \(B \cdot L\). Here a streaming kernel controls both factors, \(W\) warps per SM and \(K\) independent 16-byte loads per thread, so

$$B(W,K) = \min\!\Big(B_{\text{peak}},\; \frac{20 \cdot W \cdot K \cdot 512\ \text{bytes}}{L_{\text{DRAM}}}\Big).$$
fig. 2  achieved bandwidth against bytes in flight, 36 configurations

Figure 2. ·

Plotted against bytes in flight, configurations with very different W and K fall close to one curve, which is the content of the law. Two things depart from it. The model is consistently optimistic in the latency-bound region, by 12 to 17%, and one warp per SM never passes about 87 GB/s however many loads each thread issues (the two hollow points below the plateau). §9 shows the second is a property of the loop, not the hardware, while the first remains open.

§4Coalescing is a sector count

Memory is moved in 32-byte sectors. When the 32 lanes of a warp read 4-byte values at a stride of \(S\) elements, the request touches \(\min(32, 4S)\) sectors, but only 128 bytes of them are used. The useful bandwidth is therefore

$$B_{\text{useful}}(S) = \frac{B_{\text{peak}}}{\min(8, S)} .$$
fig. 3  useful bandwidth against stride

Figure 3. Points are measurements and the line is the sector model, with no fitted parameter. Hardware counters (Table 1) show the mechanism directly.

Table 1. Nsight Compute counters for the stride kernel. Sectors per request and DRAM bytes per useful byte match the model exactly. The extra 11% lost at stride 64 is not explained by sectors, since the counters there are the same as at stride 8. §9 traces it to the 512 MB span of this test.

§5The roofline, traced by one kernel

The roofline model[4] says a kernel runs at \(\min(P_{\text{peak}}, I \cdot B_{\text{peak}})\), where \(I\) is its arithmetic intensity in FLOP per byte. Usually it is drawn from many different kernels. Here one kernel traces it. Each thread loads a 16-byte vector and applies \(F\) fused multiply-adds to every element, so \(I = 2F/\text{sizeof}(T)\), and sweeping \(F\) from 1 to 512 walks along the roof.

fig. 4  one kernel swept across arithmetic intensity
FP32FP64lines: model at the clock measured in each run

Figure 4. ·

FP32 follows the roof to within a few percent and reaches 98% of its peak. FP64 does not. It levels off at 84% of the two-lanes-per-SM model, at both clock states. The divergence experiment in the next section measures the same 84% from a completely different kernel, so the shortfall belongs to the FP64 unit, not to this benchmark. §9 measures where the missing cycles go.

§6Divergence costs paths, not lanes

The 32 lanes of a warp share one instruction stream. When lanes disagree at a branch, the warp runs each side in turn with the other lanes masked off. In this kernel the first \(a\) lanes are active and lane \(\ell\) runs path \(\ell \bmod p\), each path a separate function so the compiler cannot merge them. The active lanes then take \(\min(a, p)\) distinct paths, and the model is

$$T(a, p) = c_1 \cdot \min(a, p).$$
fig. 5  cycles per FP64 instruction per SM, 16 combinations of active lanes and paths

Figure 5. ·

One active lane costs exactly as much as 32. What costs is the number of paths. This is the effect that halved the FP64 throughput of the smoother in investigation 02, where a red-black branch left more than half of every warp idle.

§7Banks, and a limit that is not banks

Shared memory is split into 32 banks of 4 bytes, and each bank serves one word per cycle. If lane \(\ell\) reads word \(\ell S\), the bank is \(\ell S \bmod 32\) and the number of lanes landing on the busiest bank is \(\gcd(S, 32)\). The textbook prediction is that time is proportional to \(\gcd(S, 32)\).

fig. 6  cycles per shared-memory warp load against stride

Figure 6. ·

Above 2 cycles the prediction is exact, and stride 33, the usual padding trick, is back at full speed. Below 2 it fails. A conflict-free 32-bit load still takes 2 cycles per warp, although the counters report a single pass through the banks. So the limit there is not the banks. 32-bit shared loads can only issue once every 2 cycles per SM, which is 64 bytes per cycle, half of what the banks deliver. Wider loads prove it. Eight bytes per lane move 128 bytes in the same 2 cycles, the full bank bandwidth. The model that fits all three widths is

$$T = \max(2,\ \text{wavefronts}),$$

where wavefronts are counted separately for each group of lanes that fills 128 bytes, 32 lanes at 4 bytes, 16 at 8 and 8 at 16. One mistake is worth recording. The first wide-load kernel used ordinary C++ loads, the compiler hoisted the few distinct addresses out of the loop, and the kernel reported an impossible 178 bytes per cycle. The loads are now explicit ld.volatile.shared instructions.

§9Following up the gaps

Each gap had several candidate explanations. For each one I ran a further experiment chosen so that the candidates predict different outcomes.

FP64 runs at 19 cycles per instruction

W warps per SM run either one dependent FMA chain per thread or eight independent chains. If the shortfall came from too little parallelism, more warps or more chains would close it. They do not.

fig. 7  cycles per FP64 warp instruction per SM against warps per SM
one dependent chaineight independent chains

Figure 7. ·

A dependent FMA takes · cycles, and the SM never issues FP64 instructions faster than one per · cycles. Little's law, applied this time to an arithmetic unit, says \(49/19 \approx 2.6\) warps of dependent work are needed to hide the latency, and the floor indeed appears at 3. The counters show the FP64 pipe busy for exactly 16 of those 19 cycles in every configuration. The waiting warps are stalled on the short scoreboard, the mechanism the SM uses for variable-latency units such as shared memory, and almost never on the math pipe itself. On this chip FP64 behaves like such a unit, and each instruction leaves the pipe idle for 3 cycles, independent of clock, occupancy and dependency structure. That is the 84% of §5, now measured in cycles. Why the hardware needs those 3 cycles cannot be seen from software.

The single-warp cap was the loop

The streaming loop waits for all K loads, sums them, and only then issues the next K. A software-pipelined version issues round \(n+1\) before summing round \(n\). With one warp per SM it reaches · GB/s at K = 8, the full peak, where the plain loop stops short. The cap in §3 was the code, not a hardware limit on outstanding requests. The 12 to 17% shortfall at K = 1 did not move under pipelining, so serial work in the loop is not its cause. It also appears for L2-resident data, and latency-bound runs vary by up to 25% between sessions while bandwidth-bound runs repeat to 1%. That remains open.

The 64 MB step is a translation cache

To isolate address translation, one thread chases through one 128-byte line in each block of \(g\) bytes, at a random offset within the block, with loads that bypass L1. The data then always sits in L2, so the baseline is the L2 latency, and only translation can add to it. Varying \(g\) separates the number of entries from the page size.

fig. 8  L2-hit latency against the number of 2 MB pages touched

Figure 8. ·

For every block of 2 MB or more, the step comes at exactly 17 blocks, so the cache holds 16 entries. For 64 KB blocks it comes when the span passes \(16 \times 2\ \text{MB} = 32\ \text{MB}\), so each entry covers 2 MB. Larger pages would let 17 blocks of 2 MB share 9 entries, and smaller pages would make 64 KB blocks step at 17, so the page size is exactly 2 MB. A miss costs about 10 cycles, too little for a page-table walk, so a second, larger level must answer it. The 64 KB curve rises gradually instead of in one step, and that fits too. There the chase revisits pages in random order, so with 16 entries a load finds its page cached with probability \(16/P\), which predicts +5.0, +7.5 and +8.8 cycles at 32, 64 and 128 pages against +5.6, +8.0 and +10.3 measured. This accounts for the step in Figure 1. The stride-64 loss of §4 turned out to belong to the 512 MB span of that test, since at a fixed 128 MB span stride 64 runs as fast as its neighbours.

Still open

Three things remain unexplained. The Little's-law shortfall at low concurrency, together with its run-to-run variation, which may come from the memory clock changing under light load. Strides of 96, 192 and 256 elements, which at a fixed span lose 35 to 55% of their bandwidth while 128 does not, probably through the undocumented mapping of addresses to memory channels. And the hardware reason for the 3 idle FP64 cycles.
